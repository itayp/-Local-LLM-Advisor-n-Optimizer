A Year on the Allotment

The allotments sit on a strip of land between the railway cutting and the back gardens of Station Road, about forty plots in all, each one roughly the size of a tennis court. A chain-link fence runs along the railway side, and a row of old hawthorns softens the other edge. There is a single gate, a water trough fed from the mains, a shed for the committee's tools, and a noticeboard that has carried the same faded rota for the bonfire since anyone can remember. Most people come on foot. A few arrive by bicycle with a spade strapped along the crossbar, and on Saturday mornings there is usually a queue of cars parked half on the verge, boots open, unloading seed trays and bags of compost.

I took on plot twenty-three in the middle of February, which everyone told me was either the best time or the worst time to start, depending on who was talking. The previous tenant had moved away the summer before, and nobody had touched the ground since. What had been neat rows of leeks and a strawberry bed was now a mat of couch grass, bindweed and dock, with a collapsed bean frame in one corner and a sheet of corrugated iron that turned out to be sheltering a family of toads. The secretary of the allotment society walked me round it, pointed out the boundary pegs, and said that if I could get half of it under control by the end of April I would be doing better than most.

The first weeks were mostly about clearing. I had imagined a tidy process, like peeling an orange, but it was closer to archaeology. Every forkful of soil brought up something: broken pots, a length of hosepipe, a child's plastic dinosaur, bottle tops by the dozen, and roots, always roots, white and brittle and endless. Couch grass spreads by underground runners that snap when you pull them, and each fragment left behind can grow into a new plant. The older plot holders have strong opinions about how to deal with it. Some dig it out by hand, a square metre at a time, and sieve the soil. Others cover the ground with black sheeting for a whole season and let the darkness do the work. A few reach for weedkiller and do not talk about it.

I chose a mixture, which is to say I chose the slowest method for half the plot and the laziest method for the rest. The half nearest the path I dug by hand over six weekends, turning each spit of soil, shaking out the roots and throwing them onto a growing pile to dry in the wind. The far half I covered with old carpet donated by a neighbour and weighed down with bricks. It looked terrible. It also meant that by the start of spring I had a patch of clean, dark, crumbly ground to plant in, and a larger patch that I could forget about for a while.

Soil is the thing nobody warns you about properly. People talk about seeds and varieties and when to sow, but everything depends on what is underneath. Ours is heavy clay over a layer of old railway ballast, and in winter it holds water like a basin. When it dries in summer it sets as hard as a pavement and cracks into plates. The only real cure is organic matter, added year after year: manure, compost, leaf mould, anything that once lived. The society gets a delivery of well-rotted horse manure from a stables on the edge of town every autumn, dumped in a steaming heap by the gate, and there is a quiet scramble to barrow it away before it is gone. I missed that delivery by several months and had to buy bags of compost from the garden centre instead, which felt a little like cheating.

The weather in early March was cold and bright. On still mornings a frost lay across the plots and the puddles along the main path froze at the edges. There was not much to do outside beyond digging, so I spent the evenings at the kitchen table with seed catalogues, drawing plans on squared paper. The plans changed every night. One week the whole plot was going to be potatoes, because a man on plot nine said potatoes break up the ground better than any spade. The next week it was going to be a proper rotation, divided into four beds, with brassicas following legumes following roots following potatoes, the way the books describe it. In the end I settled on something in between, which is how most plans on an allotment end up.

A rotation matters because different crops take different things from the soil and leave different problems behind. Peas and beans, for example, carry bacteria in small nodules on their roots that capture nitrogen from the air, so the ground they leave is richer than before. Cabbages and their relatives are hungry for that nitrogen and do well after them. Potatoes and tomatoes share diseases that linger in the soil, so growing them in the same place year after year invites trouble. Onions, carrots and parsnips prefer ground that has not been freshly manured, because rich soil makes their roots fork and split. None of this is complicated once it is written down, but it takes a season or two before it becomes second nature.

By the middle of March the windowsills at home were full. Tomatoes, peppers and a few aubergines went into small pots on the sunniest sill, because they need a long warm start that our climate will not give them outside. Leeks went into a deep tray, sown thinly, to grow on until they were the thickness of a pencil. Sweet peas went into cardboard tubes from kitchen rolls, which let their long roots grow straight down and can be planted whole later on. The house began to smell faintly of damp compost, and my partner started to ask, reasonably, how many more trays were coming.

Out on the plot, the first job of spring was the potatoes. Seed potatoes arrive in late winter and need a few weeks of chitting before they go in the ground, which means standing them in egg boxes in a cool, light room until short, dark green shoots appear. The shoots should be sturdy and stubby, not long and pale; long pale shoots mean the room is too warm and too dark. I planted the first earlies on a grey afternoon at the end of March, in trenches about a spade's depth, a foot apart, with the shoots pointing up. Then I raked the soil over them and stood back feeling that something had begun.

April is the month when the allotments come back to life. People who have not been seen since October reappear with new tools and good intentions. The water trough is switched back on. Someone repaints the gate. The robins, which have followed every spade all winter, are joined by blackbirds and thrushes working the freshly turned soil for worms. On a warm day you can hear several radios at once from different plots, each tuned to a different station, and the smell of the first grass cuttings drifts over from the gardens behind. It is also the month of false starts. A week of sunshine tempts everyone to sow too early, and then a sharp frost undoes half their work.

I learned the frost lesson the hard way. After a warm spell in the second week of April I planted out a row of lettuce seedlings and sowed a line of French beans directly into the ground, against the advice of the man on plot nine, who said the soil was still too cold and the nights too long. Two nights later the temperature fell below freezing. The lettuces survived, looking bruised and sulky, but the bean seeds rotted in the cold wet ground without ever coming up. The man on plot nine was kind enough not to mention it, although he did lend me a thermometer and suggest I push it into the soil before sowing anything that likes warmth.

The thermometer turned out to be one of the most useful things on the plot. Most seeds have a temperature below which they simply sit in the soil and wait, or rot. Peas and broad beans are content in cool ground. Carrots and parsnips are slow in the cold and faster in the warm. Beans, squashes, sweetcorn and cucumbers want the soil at ten or twelve degrees at least, which on our clay does not happen until well into May. Once I started checking the soil rather than the calendar, the sowing went much better, and the rows came up evenly instead of in scattered clumps.

There is a social side to the allotments that I had not expected. People talk over the fences and across the paths, mostly about the weather, the slugs, and what the committee is or is not doing about the rabbits. Seeds and seedlings change hands constantly. When somebody's tomatoes germinate too well, the surplus plants are left on an upturned crate by the gate with a sign that says help yourself. Cuttings, spare canes, lengths of netting, old window frames for cold frames: everything circulates. Within a month I had been given two rhubarb crowns, a bundle of hazel poles, a bag of garlic cloves and more advice than I could possibly follow.

The advice is worth its own paragraph. Almost every plot holder has a method they believe in completely, and the methods contradict one another. One neighbour swears by planting according to the phases of the moon. Another insists that the secret to carrots is sowing them with a pinch of sand. A retired teacher on plot thirty grows everything in raised beds framed with scaffolding boards and never digs at all, while the couple next to her double-dig their entire plot every autumn with a devotion that looks like penance. The strange thing is that all of them grow good vegetables. After a while I came to believe that attention matters more than method, and that the people who do well are simply the people who are there, looking, most days of the week.

By the end of April the carpet on the far half had done its work. When I peeled it back, the grass beneath was yellow and thin, and the soil underneath was soft and full of worms. The bindweed had not given up, of course; its pale shoots had crept sideways looking for light and were waiting at the edges. But the ground could be forked over in an afternoon rather than a month, and I pulled the bindweed roots out as I went. That half became the home of the squashes, the sweetcorn and the runner beans, the crops that would go in last, when the weather was reliably warm.

Runner beans need something tall to climb. The traditional frame is a double row of canes or poles, leaning in to cross at the top, tied along a horizontal pole to make an inverted V. I built mine from the hazel poles I had been given, lashed with garden twine, and it leaned slightly to one side from the start. The man on plot nine said it would do. The beans themselves went in at the end of May, one at the foot of each pole, with a second sown alongside in case the first failed. Within a fortnight the young plants had found their poles and begun to twine upwards, always turning the same way around the support, anticlockwise when seen from above.

May brought the slugs. They had been there all along, of course, sheltering under stones and in the long grass by the fence, but the combination of warm nights and tender young plants brought them out in force. A row of lettuces could be reduced to stumps overnight. Seedlings of beans and squashes vanished, leaving only a silvery trail across the soil to show where they had gone. Every plot holder has a theory about slugs, and most of them involve beer. Shallow traps of beer sunk into the soil do catch a great many, which is either satisfying or depressing depending on your temperament. Other people use copper tape, crushed eggshells, wool pellets, or late-night patrols with a torch and a bucket.

I tried most of these and concluded that no single method works, but several together help. The most effective thing, in the end, was growing seedlings on at home until they were larger and tougher before planting them out, so that a few holes in the leaves did not matter. A clean plot helps as well: fewer hiding places near the crops means fewer slugs among them. And the frogs and toads that lived under the corrugated iron did their part. I moved the sheet to the corner by the compost bins and left it there on purpose, a small shelter for the creatures that eat the creatures that eat the lettuces.

The compost heap deserves a mention of its own, because it quietly became the centre of the whole operation. I built two bays from old pallets, wired together at the corners, with a third added later. Everything soft went in: weeds without seeds, grass cuttings, vegetable peelings from home, torn cardboard, the spent compost from seed trays. The trick, which I learned slowly, is balance. Too much green material, like grass, and the heap turns into a slimy, smelly mass. Too much brown material, like cardboard and straw, and it sits there doing nothing for a year. Mixed roughly half and half, kept damp but not wet, and turned once in a while, it heats up and breaks down within a few months into something dark and sweet-smelling that the soil loves.

There is something very satisfying about compost. It turns the waste of one season into the fertility of the next, and it asks for almost nothing in return. On cold mornings in late spring you can push your hand into the middle of a working heap and feel real warmth, the heat of countless microbes breaking the material down. Worms move up from the soil beneath in their thousands. Once, lifting the top layer with a fork, I found a slow worm curled in the warm debris, a legless lizard the colour of polished bronze, which slid away into the pallets before I could look properly.

June is the month when the plot begins to look like a garden rather than a building site. The potatoes were in full leaf, and I earthed them up, drawing soil from between the rows up around the stems so that the new tubers would stay covered and not turn green in the light. The peas were climbing their netting and beginning to flower, white and faintly scented. The onions, which had gone in as small sets in March, were swelling at the base. The first broad beans were ready to pick, and I ate a handful raw, straight from the pod, standing in the middle of the path in the evening sun.

Watering became the main job. Our clay soil holds moisture well in spring, but by midsummer it dries from the top down, and shallow-rooted crops like lettuces and radishes suffer first. The mains trough by the gate is a long walk with two full cans, and the rules forbid hosepipes, for good reason. I learned to water less often but more deeply, soaking the ground around each plant rather than sprinkling the surface, which encourages roots to grow down where the soil stays cool. Mulching helped as well: a layer of grass cuttings or compost laid over the damp soil kept it from drying out and smothered weeds at the same time.

Weeds, of course, never stopped. Once the soil warmed up, a new crop of seedlings appeared every week, hairy bittercress and groundsel and chickweed and fat hen, and in the corners the bindweed kept sending up its twisting shoots. The advice I was given most often was to hoe little and often, on a dry day, so that the tiny weeds wither where they are cut. A sharp hoe run between the rows for ten minutes every few days does more good than a whole Saturday spent on hands and knees once the weeds have got away. I did not always follow that advice, but when I did, it worked.

Midsummer also brought the first real harvests, and with them a new problem: too much of everything at once. The lettuces all hearted up in the same week. The radishes went from perfect to woody in what seemed like a single afternoon. The courgettes, which had sat sulking for weeks, suddenly began producing a fruit a day per plant, and if one was missed under the leaves it turned into a marrow the size of a rolling pin. Every plot holder at the allotments faces the same glut at the same time, which is why the crate by the gate fills up with free courgettes in July and nobody takes them.

The answer to gluts, I was told, is succession sowing: small amounts, sown every two or three weeks, so that crops mature in turn rather than all together. It sounds simple and requires a kind of discipline I did not have in my first year. By July I was sowing short rows of lettuce, beetroot and carrots every fortnight, and by late summer that steady supply was arriving as planned. The earlier gluts went to neighbours, friends, colleagues at work, and a food bank in the town that takes fresh vegetables on Tuesday mornings.

The tomatoes, which had spent the spring on the windowsill and then in pots in a sheltered corner, went out into their final positions in early June, tied to tall canes. Tomatoes grown this way need their side shoots pinched out, the small shoots that appear where a leaf joins the main stem, so that the plant puts its energy into fruit rather than more leaves. Miss a week and the plant becomes a tangled bush. They also need regular feeding once the first flowers set, with a liquid feed high in potash. Some people make their own from comfrey leaves steeped in water for a few weeks, which works well and smells indescribably bad.

Comfrey grows at the end of almost every older plot at the allotments. It is a tall, bristly plant with drooping purple or cream flowers, and its deep roots draw up minerals from far below the reach of most crops. The leaves can be cut several times a year and added to the compost heap, laid as a mulch around plants, or steeped to make that notorious liquid feed. I was given a root cutting by the retired teacher on plot thirty, with a warning that once comfrey is established it is almost impossible to remove, so I should plant it somewhere I would never want to grow anything else. It went in by the compost bays, and within a year it was waist high.

By July the runner beans had reached the top of their poles and were flowering, scarlet against the green, and the bees were working them from morning to evening. Bumblebees in particular seem to love beans, and I spent more time than I would like to admit sitting on an upturned bucket watching them. The allotments are full of insects in summer: hoverflies over the flowers, ladybirds on the broad beans hunting aphids, butterflies laying eggs on the cabbages, which is less welcome. A few plot holders keep hives in the far corner by the railway, and in a good year the society sells jars of honey at the autumn show.

Not every insect is a friend. The cabbages, which I had planted out in late spring under a low tunnel of fine netting, were safe from the white butterflies for as long as the netting stayed tight. The one week it came loose at a corner, a butterfly found its way in, and a fortnight later the leaves were covered with small green caterpillars that could strip a plant to its ribs in days. I picked them off by hand every morning for weeks. The blackfly on the broad beans were easier: pinching out the soft tips of the plants, where the aphids gather, removed most of them, and the ladybirds dealt with the rest.

The first early potatoes were ready in the second week of July, when the plants had flowered and the lowest leaves were beginning to yellow. Lifting potatoes is the closest thing on an allotment to finding treasure. You push the fork in well away from the stem, lever the whole plant up, and the tubers tumble out of the loosened soil, pale and clean and far more numerous than seems possible from the single seed potato planted in March. I dug the first row with my partner, who had until then been politely sceptical about the whole enterprise, and we boiled a pan of them that evening with mint from the edge of the plot. They were the best potatoes either of us had eaten.

There is a reason people say that home-grown vegetables taste better, and it is not only sentiment. Many vegetables begin to change the moment they are picked. Sweetcorn and peas convert their sugars to starch within hours, which is why corn eaten straight from the plant tastes so much sweeter than corn from a shop. Salad leaves wilt and lose their crispness. Tomatoes picked ripe from the vine have a depth of flavour that tomatoes picked green and ripened in storage never quite reach. Growing your own also means choosing varieties for flavour rather than for how well they survive a lorry journey, and some of the old varieties passed around the allotments have been grown locally for generations.

Seed saving is a quiet tradition on our site. A handful of plot holders save their own seed every year from the best plants: beans left on the plant until the pods dry and rattle, tomatoes squeezed into a jar and left to ferment before the seeds are rinsed and dried, lettuces allowed to bolt and flower until the seed heads turn to fluff. There is an old runner bean variety on plot four, with speckled purple seeds, that the family who hold the plot say came from a great-grandfather who worked on the railway. They give a few seeds to every new tenant, and I planted mine at the end of my own row, where they outgrew everything else.

August on the allotments is slower and hotter. The early rush of sowing and planting is over, and most of the work is picking, watering and keeping up with the weeds. The paths are dusty, and the grass along the fence turns straw coloured. People come early in the morning or late in the evening to avoid the heat, and on some evenings there are more people sitting outside their sheds with a flask of tea than actually working. The light at that time of day is extraordinary, low and golden across the rows, and the swifts scream overhead on their way back to the eaves of the houses along Station Road.

The squashes, which had taken weeks to get going, finally went wild in August. A single winter squash plant can send out runners several metres long, rooting where they touch the ground, and mine crossed the path into the neighbouring plot before I noticed. The neighbour, a young family who had taken on plot twenty-two in the spring, did not seem to mind and claimed any fruit that formed on their side. The sweetcorn, planted in a block rather than a row so that the wind could carry pollen between the plants, stood taller than me by the end of the month, the tassels at the top shedding yellow dust and the silks at the cobs turning from green to brown.

Knowing when sweetcorn is ready is a small skill that everyone has a trick for. The silks should be dark and dry, and when you peel back a little of the husk and press a kernel with a thumbnail, the juice should be milky rather than watery or doughy. Watery means too early, doughy means too late. I got it right about half the time. The rule on our site is that the pan of water should be boiling before you go out to pick the cobs, and although that is an exaggeration, it is not much of one.

At the end of August the allotment society holds its annual show in the church hall across the road. It is a small affair, a dozen trestle tables covered in white paper, with classes for the longest runner bean, the heaviest marrow, five matched onions, a plate of three tomatoes, a jar of chutney and a vase of sweet peas, among others. Judging is done by a retired nurseryman from a neighbouring village who takes it extremely seriously, examining the onions for blemishes with a magnifying glass. Rivalries that simmer all year come to the surface. The same three people have won the onion class for as long as anyone can remember, and they do not share their methods.

I entered the sweet peas, mostly because they were the only thing I had grown that looked presentable, and came third out of four. The card said the stems were a little short. My partner framed the card, which I thought was taking things too far, but it now hangs in the shed on the plot next to a calendar and a list of what was sown when. The show ends with tea and cake and a raffle, and the proceeds go towards the manure delivery in the autumn. This time I made sure to put my name down for a share of it.

September brings a change in the light and in the work. The mornings are cooler, dew lies heavily on the grass, and spiders' webs stretch across every gap between the canes. The main crops are coming to an end: the runner beans slow down, the tomato plants begin to yellow, and the potatoes for storage are lifted and left to dry on the surface for a few hours before being put into paper sacks. The onions, which had been harvested in August and laid out to dry on an old bed frame, are plaited into strings or stored in net bags hung from the shed roof. It is the season of storing and putting away.

Storage turned out to be a subject as large as growing. Potatoes need darkness, cool and dry air, and to be kept away from frost. Squashes and pumpkins need the opposite: warmth and dryness for a couple of weeks to harden their skins, and then a cool room where they will keep for months. Carrots and beetroot can be left in the ground and dug as needed, or packed in boxes of damp sand. Apples, from the old tree in the far corner of the site that nobody owns and everyone picks from, keep best wrapped individually in newspaper and laid out so that they do not touch. Our spare room smelled of apples and earth all winter.

Autumn is also the time to plant for the next year. Garlic goes in during October or November, individual cloves pushed into the soil with their pointed ends up, because it needs a spell of cold to split into proper bulbs. Broad beans of the hardy varieties can be sown in late autumn to overwinter as small plants and crop early the following summer. Onion sets of the overwintering kind go in at the same time. There is something hopeful about planting in autumn, when the rest of the plot is being cleared and cut back, as if you are making a promise to yourself that you will still be there in the spring.

Clearing the plot in October is a long job but not an unpleasant one. The spent bean plants are cut at the base, leaving the roots in the soil with their nitrogen nodules, and the stems go onto the compost heap. The canes and poles are pulled up, cleaned and stacked in the shed. Beds that will not be used over winter are covered with a thick layer of manure or compost, or sown with a green manure, a quick-growing crop such as field beans or winter rye that protects the soil from heavy rain and can be dug in during the spring. Bare soil left exposed to the winter weather loses its structure and its nutrients, washed away by rain that the roots of a cover crop would have held.

The manure arrived in the first week of November, as promised, a huge dark heap by the gate that steamed in the cold air. This time I was there with a wheelbarrow on the first morning, along with half the site. It is hard, heavy work, barrowing load after load along the main path, and by the end of the day my shoulders ached in a way they had not since the digging in February. But it felt like an investment. Spread over the beds and left for the worms to take down through the winter, the manure would open up the clay and feed the next year's crops. The soil I had been fighting in February was slowly becoming the soil I wanted.

November and December are the quietest months. The days are short, and in the week there is rarely enough daylight after work to do anything on the plot. At weekends there are still jobs: leeks and parsnips to dig, the last cabbages to cut, the Brussels sprouts to pick from the bottom of the stem upwards as the buttons firm up. Parsnips are said to taste better after a frost, which turns some of their starch into sugar, and on our site there is a tradition of leaving them in the ground until Christmas and digging them on the morning of the holiday. The ground was frozen solid the year I tried it, and I had to pour a kettle of hot water along the row before the fork would go in.

Winter on the allotments has its own pleasures. With the leaves down, you can see across the whole site, from the railway cutting to the back fences of the houses. The robins are tame enough to perch on the handle of a fork while you work. Frost picks out every seed head and cobweb in white. There are fewer people about, and those who come tend to stay longer, talking by the shed doors about what went well and what went badly, and what they will do differently next year. It is when most of the real learning happens, I think, looking back rather than forward.

Looking back on my own first year, the list of failures is long. The early beans rotted, the carrots were forked and hairy because I sowed them in freshly manured soil, the cabbages were eaten by caterpillars, and a whole row of beetroot bolted in a dry spell without ever forming proper roots. The celery never got going. The melons, which I had grown against all advice because a catalogue made them look easy, produced two fruits the size of tennis balls that never ripened. The list of successes is shorter but sweeter: the potatoes, the runner beans, the tomatoes, the squashes, the sweet peas, and the rhubarb, which asks for nothing and gives generously every spring.

What surprised me most was how much of the year was spent not growing things but preparing to grow them: clearing, digging, composting, building frames, raising seedlings, mending netting, sharpening tools. The harvest is the part people photograph, but it is the smallest part of the work. The rest is maintenance, patience and a steady willingness to put right what the weather, the slugs and your own mistakes have undone. I had expected the allotment to be a hobby. It turned out to be closer to a practice, something you get better at only by doing it regularly, whether or not you feel like it on a given day.

In January the seed catalogues arrived again, and the kitchen table disappeared under them for a second winter. This time the plans were more modest and, I hoped, more realistic. Fewer varieties, grown better. Carrots in a bed that had not been manured, with a fine mesh over them against the carrot fly, which finds its target by scent and flies close to the ground. Cabbages under netting that was properly pegged down at every corner. French beans sown in pots at home and planted out only when the soil thermometer said the ground was warm enough. The melons were crossed off the list without discussion.

January is also the month for cleaning and mending. The tools came home one at a time to be scrubbed, dried, sharpened and oiled. A spade with a sharp edge cuts through clay and roots with half the effort of a blunt one, and a hoe that has been filed properly slices weeds off at the surface instead of dragging them along. The wooden handles got a rub of linseed oil. The pallets of the compost bays, which had begun to rot where they touched the ground, were propped up on bricks. The shed roof, which had leaked all autumn onto the seed tins, was finally covered with a new sheet of felt, nailed down on a cold, still Saturday with the help of the man on plot nine.

Rhubarb is the first crop of the new year, and on our site it is forced in the traditional way. In the middle of winter, an upturned bucket or an old chimney pot is placed over a crown to shut out the light. Deprived of light, the plant sends up long, tender, pale pink stalks weeks before the ordinary crop, sweeter and less stringy than the green stalks of spring. The forced stalks are ready in late February or March, just when there is almost nothing else to pick, and a crumble made from them tastes like the first sign that winter is ending. A crown that has been forced needs a year or two to recover, so the buckets move around the rhubarb bed in turn.

By the second February, the anniversary of taking on the plot, the ground looked very different from the mat of couch grass I had started with. The beds were dark with manure. The paths between them were covered in wood chips from a tree surgeon who dumps loads at the gate for anyone who wants them. The garlic was up, a row of stiff green shoots, and the overwintered broad beans stood a few inches high, a little battered by the wind but alive. The corrugated iron was still in its corner, and when I lifted it to check, the toads were still there, dozing in the damp.

There were new tenants that winter as well. Plot sixteen, which had been abandoned for years, was taken on by two students from the college in town who arrived with enormous enthusiasm and not a single tool between them. Within a week they had been given a spade, two forks, a rake, a pile of canes and a bundle of seed packets, and the man on plot nine had walked them round their boundary pegs and told them that if they could get half of it under control by the end of April they would be doing better than most. I recognised the speech. I found myself offering them a rhubarb crown and more advice than they could possibly follow.

That, in the end, may be the real lesson of the allotments. They are not a collection of forty separate gardens but a small community that happens to be arranged in plots. Knowledge moves along the paths from the people who have been there longest to the people who arrived last, in the form of seeds and cuttings and tools and stories. The same questions are asked every spring and answered patiently every spring. The same mistakes are made, and forgiven, and made again by someone else. And the ground itself carries on underneath all of it, slowly improving under the care of whoever holds the plot this year, and waiting for whoever holds it next.

This year I have begun to keep a proper diary of the plot, a plain notebook that lives in the shed next to the calendar and the framed third-place card. Every visit gets a line or two: the date, the weather, the temperature of the soil, what was sown, what was picked, what went wrong. It is not very interesting to read, and nobody else will ever read it. But it means that next spring I will not have to rely on memory to know when the first frost came, which week the slugs arrived, or how long the parsnips took to come up. The diary begins in February, on a cold, bright morning, with the first spade of the second season going into the ground.
